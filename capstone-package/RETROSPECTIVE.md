# Retrospective — for the person I was in Week 1

Ten weeks ago, my proof statement was simple: build a model that tells a content team which declining page to look at first. I remember thinking the hard part would be the modeling — picking the right algorithm, tuning it, getting a good score. I was wrong about where the hard part was, and that's most of what this retrospective is about.

**What I set out to do.** Rank a content portfolio by decline risk, using FlyRank's real (anonymized) search-performance data, and hand a content strategist a shortlist instead of a spreadsheet. By Week 5 I had that: a logistic regression, a grouped train/test split by `client_id` so no client leaked between the two, and a model that scored precision, recall, and accuracy of exactly 1.000.

**What changed.** That 1.000 is the pivot of the whole project, and it's not a modeling story — it's a trust story. Week 1 me would have shipped that number. Week 6 me looked at it and felt the specific unease of a result that's too good, ran the leakage-hunting checklist, and found two features — `impressions_last_30d` and `impressions_prev_30d` — whose coefficients nearly canceled at -7.92 and +7.91. That's the fingerprint of a feature that's mathematically tied to the label rather than genuinely predictive of it. I didn't just take the checklist's word for it: I ran the confession test myself, retraining once with those features and once without, and watched accuracy collapse from 1.0 to about 0.67. Watching a number I wanted to be true fall apart under a test I designed to break it is the single moment that changed how I work. The honest model that came out the other side — 71.5% global precision, 89% precision in the top 100 of the ranked queue — is a smaller number than 1.000, and it's the only one I actually trust.

Claude was part of that pivot, not incidental to it. It helped me apply the leakage checklist and recognize the canceling-coefficient pattern, but the verification — the confession test, the before/after numbers, deciding the fix was real — was mine to run and mine to check. That division of labor is the thing I didn't have a name for in Week 1 and now do: AI as a second reviewer that's very good at pattern-matching to known failure modes, not as an oracle whose output I trust without rerunning it myself.

**What I'd build next.** Three concrete things. First, cross-validate instead of relying on a single holdout — the current precision@K numbers would move somewhat under a different random seed, and I know that because the limitations section says so, not because I've hidden it. Second, close the coverage gap: the holdout that produced precision@100/250 landed entirely on one content type, so I'd want a stratified split that actually exercises all three. Third, and most important, get this in front of a real content team and see whether the top-100 shortlist is where they'd have looked anyway, or whether it's telling them something they'd have missed — the model has never been checked against a human's actual judgment, and that's the test that would tell me if 89% precision means anything in practice.

**Three things that transferred beyond this project.** First, a suspiciously good number is a bug report, not a result — I now reflexively ask what a perfect score would have to be leaking before I let myself be pleased by it. Second, working with an AI coding partner works best as a second set of eyes that names a pattern for me to go verify myself, not as a source of conclusions I adopt directly — the confession test only meant something because I ran it and watched it fail. Third, writing for a stranger to reproduce is a different and harder skill than writing for my own memory of the last three weeks; half of what made this README rewrite slow was realizing how much context I'd been silently assuming a reader already had.

The proof statement from Week 1 is still true. It just took getting the number wrong once, in the most convincing possible way, to find out what proving it honestly actually required.
